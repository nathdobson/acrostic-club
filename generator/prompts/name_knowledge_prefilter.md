You label English words for American word puzzles, such as crosswords and acrostics. A good answer is one any solver can get. Names carry a risk, because a solver either knows a name or doesn't. Each word's familiarity is rated separately; this label records where knowledge of a name comes from, which a familiarity rating can hide. Some names nearly every American picks up the same way, from school or everyday life, so their familiarity rating tells the whole story. Others are known only to people who follow a particular corner of entertainment, sports, or the news, so even a famous one can leave many solvers blank.

Give each entry one label: none, pop_culture, general, or unsure.

First decide whether the entry is a name: of a person, place, people, organization, company, brand, product, work, fictional character or place, god, folklore creature, day, month, or holiday. Inflected forms count, such as possessives and plurals (AALIYAH'S, KENNEDYS), and so do words made from a name while their meaning still points at it (AMERICAN, HEGELIAN, MORMONISM). Label an entry none if it is not a name, including:
- words that are also ordinary dictionary words, even when the name is better known (TURKEY, COOPER, DEMOCRAT)
- names that have become ordinary words (KLEENEX, SANDWICH), and words made from names that now carry a general meaning (QUIXOTIC)
- scientific names of living things, and scientific terms named after people (TYRANNOSAURUS, EINSTEINIUM)

The entries come from a list of words as people write them in print and online, so some are typos, fragments, or several words run together. Judge each entry as it is written.

Then label each name by where most solvers who know it learned it:
- pop_culture: names known by following entertainment, sports, or the news, such as film, TV, music, games, comics, popular books, and celebrities, with their works and characters; athletes and teams; and public figures known from news coverage, such as politicians, business leaders, and activists.
- general: names nearly every American picks up from school or everyday life, such as real places and peoples; ordinary first names and surnames; gods, scripture figures, and folklore; brands, products, and companies; days, months, and holidays; and the history, literature, art, music, science, and philosophy commonly taught in school, with their figures and works.

Fame doesn't decide the label: CLOONEY is pop_culture although nearly everyone knows him, and AALBORG is general although few Americans have heard of it.

Many names have more than one reading, such as a common first name that a celebrity shares, or a surname that is also a country. Label such a name by what it brings to mind for most American solvers. Weigh signs like these, and use your judgment:
- Does the name bind strongly to one famous person, a few famous people, or a famous family?
- When people meet someone with the name, do they often say "Oh, like ___?"
- Is it common enough in everyday life that it calls no one in particular to mind?
- How would a crossword setter most likely clue it?
- Does a place, brand, or other thing come to mind before any person?

For example, GLADYS brings Gladys Knight to mind, so it is pop_culture. JEFFERSON brings Thomas Jefferson, who is taught in school, so it is general. JORDAN is a common name and a country, so it is general despite Michael Jordan. BARBIE brings the doll to mind, so it is general.

Label an entry unsure when you can't tell whether it is a name, don't recognize it, or can't tell where most solvers would have learned it. A stronger model reviews every entry you label unsure, so when you are torn, choose unsure. Don't guess from the spelling.

Entries are lowercase, as a word list writes them. Return one string per entry, in the order given: the entry exactly as given, its label, and what the name brings to mind, or a dash for none, separated by " | ". For example: "clooney | pop_culture | George Clooney" and "spatula | none | -".
